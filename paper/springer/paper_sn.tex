\title[Differential Fault Analysis of Dipper]{Differential Fault Analysis of Dipper: The Role of Modular Addition}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Ali} \sur{Huseynli}}\email{ali.huseynli@student.aztu.edu.az}
\author[1]{\fnm{Yadigar} \sur{Imamverdiyev}}
% NOTE (Ali): add Yadigar Imamverdiyev's e-mail (\email{...}) and both ORCIDs if the journal asks.

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Cybersecurity}, \orgname{Azerbaijan Technical University}, \orgaddress{\city{Baku}, \country{Azerbaijan}}}

\abstract{Dipper is a 64-bit lightweight block cipher with 96- and 128-bit keys that combines the
GIFT S-box and bit permutation with modular additions of 16-bit words. We study its resistance to
differential fault analysis (DFA) in software simulation. In the main fault model a random nibble
of the S-box input of one round is changed by an unknown non-zero value. Round keys are recovered
from the last round backwards with a key filter that guesses two key bits at a time, from the least
significant bit upwards, and carries the borrow of the modular subtraction. The master key then
follows from the inverse key schedule. Over 1000 random keys per setting, the full 96-bit key was
recovered in every trial, with a median of 210 faulty ciphertexts when 16 candidates are left and
of 116 or 91 faults when $2^{8}$ or $2^{12}$ candidates are left for a final check. For the 128-bit
key, a median of 208 faults plus a $2^{32}$ search, or 316 faults without a search, was needed. To
isolate the effect of the modular addition we repeated the attack on two analysis-only variants in
which the addition is replaced by XOR or removed. For these variants the fault one round before the
target key gives no information under the nibble model, while under a single-bit model the same
attack needed a median of 299 faults against 115 for Dipper. In our experiments the modular
addition therefore did not make DFA harder; it gave the attacker a key-dependent check one round
closer to the ciphertext. We also show that four bits of every round key cannot be found by this
filter, note that Dipper's last round ends with a keyless invertible layer, and discuss simple
countermeasures.}

\keywords{differential fault analysis, lightweight block cipher, Dipper, ARX, GIFT S-box, key recovery}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Lightweight block ciphers are used in small devices such as sensors, smart cards and
microcontrollers. Such devices are often physically accessible to an attacker, and their
implementations can therefore be targeted by physical attacks in addition to classical
cryptanalysis. Fault attacks are one important class~\cite{BarEl2006,Barenghi2012}: the
attacker disturbs the computation, for example by a clock glitch~\cite{Agoyan2010} or a light
pulse~\cite{Skorobogatov2002}, and observes the effect on the output. The first fault attacks targeted
public-key implementations~\cite{Boneh1997}; soon after, Biham and Shamir introduced
\emph{differential fault analysis} (DFA) for secret-key ciphers~\cite{Biham1997}. In DFA the attacker
compares a correct ciphertext with a faulty one obtained from the same plaintext and key, and uses the
difference to filter key candidates. For substitution--permutation networks, faults in the input of the
last rounds are a standard target~\cite{PiretQ2003,Tunstall2011}. DFA results are available for most
lightweight ciphers, including GIFT~\cite{Xie2021,Luo2021,Saha2018,Gao2024,Kundu2025},
PRESENT~\cite{Bagheri2013,Patranabis2017} and the ARX ciphers SIMON and
SPECK~\cite{Tupsamudre2014,Huo2015}.

Dipper~\cite{Dipper2026} is a recent hybrid SPN--ARX design. It borrows the S-box and the bit
permutation of GIFT-64~\cite{Banik2017}, itself a successor of PRESENT~\cite{Bogdanov2007}, and adds
word-wise modular additions as in ARX designs such as SPECK~\cite{Beaulieu2015}. Each round applies the
GIFT S-box to all 16 nibbles, rotates the four 16-bit words by different amounts, adds two pairs of words modulo
$2^{16}$, and applies the GIFT-64 bit permutation. The modular additions are the ARX part of the
design. The design paper evaluates differential, linear, impossible-differential
and integral attacks, but it does not consider fault attacks, and we found no published fault attack
on Dipper (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

This paper answers two questions by experiment:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How many faulty ciphertexts does a DFA need to recover the full Dipper key, for both key sizes?
\item How much does the modular addition change this cost compared with the same structure without it?
\end{enumerate}
For the second question we run the same attack code on two variants of Dipper that exist only for
this measurement: one replaces the additions by XOR and one removes them. They are not proposed as
ciphers, and no security claim is made for them.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We describe a DFA on Dipper that recovers the last round keys one at a time and then the
master key through the inverse key schedule. The key filter guesses two key bits per step and
handles the modular subtraction through a borrow bit, so that no step needs to guess a whole 16- or
32-bit word.
\item We prove that, under nibble and byte faults, four bits of each round key can never be
determined by this filter (Proposition~\ref{prop:msb}), and that the next stage of the attack
removes this ambiguity in practice.
\item We report measurements over 1000 random keys for each key size, fault model and stop rule,
including the distribution of the number of faults, the number of surviving candidates after each
fault, and the trade-off between the number of faults and the size of the final search.
\item We show that for the variants without modular addition the same attack round gives no
information under nibble and byte faults (Proposition~\ref{prop:zero}), and compare the costs of
Dipper and the variants under the three fault models.
\item We note that Dipper's last round ends with a keyless, invertible layer
(Observation~\ref{obs:last}) and discuss what this means for counting rounds.
\end{itemize}
All faults are simulated in software. The results describe what the simulation shows for the
stated fault models and numbers of trials; they make no claim about any particular device. The code,
the raw results with all random seeds, and the scripts that produce every number and figure are
publicly available (see Declarations).

\paragraph{Organisation.} Section~\ref{sec:dipper} recalls Dipper and its key schedule.
Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the fault models. Section~\ref{sec:obs} collects the structural
observations the attack relies on. Section~\ref{sec:attack} describes the attack and
Section~\ref{sec:impl} its implementation and verification. Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:secondary}
report the results, Section~\ref{sec:related} compares them with published work,
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their meaning and limits, and Section~\ref{sec:cm} discusses
countermeasures.

\section{The Dipper block cipher}\label{sec:dipper}

\subsection{Round function}

Dipper~\cite{Dipper2026} has a 64-bit state, 28 rounds and 96- or 128-bit keys. We write the state as
four 16-bit words $(A,B,C,D)$ with $A$ the most significant word. Round $i$ ($0\le i\le 27$) with
round key $\rk_i$ computes
\begin{align}
y_i &= x_i \oplus \rk_i, \qquad s = S(y_i), \qquad (A,B,C,D) = s, \label{eq:r1}\\
A_1 &= A\lll 1,\quad B_1=B\lll 4,\quad C_1=C\lll 7,\quad D_1=D\lll 11, \label{eq:r2}\\
A_2 &= A_1 \boxplus B_1,\qquad C_2 = C_1 \boxplus D_1, \label{eq:r3}\\
x_{i+1} &= P(A_2, B_1, C_2, D_1), \label{eq:r4}
\end{align}
where $S$ applies the 4-bit GIFT S-box~\cite{Banik2017} to all 16 nibbles, $\boxplus$ is addition
modulo $2^{16}$ and $P$ is the GIFT-64 bit permutation~\cite{Banik2017}. Figure~\ref{fig:round} shows one round. The plaintext is
$x_0$ and the ciphertext is $x_{28}$.

We write $F = P\circ \mathrm{ARX}\circ S$ for the keyless part of a round, so that
$x_{i+1} = F(x_i\oplus\rk_i)$. $F$ is public and invertible. In the inverse direction the additions
become subtractions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inv}
A_1 = A_2 \boxminus B_1,\qquad C_1 = C_2 \boxminus D_1,
\end{equation}
followed by the inverse rotations and the inverse S-box. Note that $B_1$ and $D_1$ pass through the
mixing layer unchanged; only $A$ and $C$ are mixed. Table~\ref{tab:notation} summarises the
notation.

\subsection{Key schedule}

The round keys are the four low words of a key state $K_0,\dots,K_5$ (96-bit key) or
$K_0,\dots,K_7$ (128-bit key): $\rk_i = K_3\Vert K_2\Vert K_1\Vert K_0$, taken before the $i$-th update.
For the 96-bit key one update maps the state to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ks96}
(K_0,\dots,K_5) \leftarrow \bigl(K_4,\ S_{7..4}(K_5),\ S_{15..12}(K_0\ggg 12),\
(K_1\ggg 2)\oplus c_i,\ S_{7..4}(K_2),\ S_{15..12}(K_3)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $S_{a..b}$ applies the S-box to bits $a..b$ of a word and $c_i$ is a 5-bit LFSR constant added
to the low bits. For the 128-bit key,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ks128}
(K_0,\dots,K_7) \leftarrow \bigl(K_0\ggg 12,\ S_{15..12}(K_1\ggg 2),\ K_4,\ S_{15..12}(K_5),\
K_6\oplus c_i,\ K_7,\ K_2,\ S_{15..12}(K_3)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Both updates are bijections: every new word is an invertible function of exactly one old word.
We implemented the inverse updates and checked them on 10\,000 random keys of each size
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

\section{Fault model and attacker}\label{sec:model}

A fault is modelled as an XOR mask added to the S-box input $y_r$ of one round $r$. We use the three
models in Table~\ref{tab:models}. Random bit, nibble and byte faults are the usual models in DFA of
nibble- and byte-oriented ciphers~\cite{PiretQ2003,Tunstall2011,Xie2021,Gao2024}; surveys of how such faults
are produced in practice and how well they follow these models are given in~\cite{BarEl2006,Barenghi2012}. The attacker knows the fault model and the round in which the fault
occurs, but neither the position nor the value of the fault.

For each fault the attacker obtains the correct and the faulty ciphertext of the same plaintext under
the same key; every fault uses a fresh random plaintext. The attacker also knows one plaintext and its
correct ciphertext, which is used only to test the final candidate master keys. The cost measure is
the number of faulty ciphertexts.

\caption{Fault models. Location and value are random and unknown to the attacker.}\label{tab:models}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Model & Fault at $y_r$ & Number of possible masks\\
\midrule
M1 (main) & one random nibble, random non-zero value & $16\times 15 = 240$\\
M2 & one random bit & 64\\
M3 & one random aligned byte (two adjacent nibbles), random non-zero value & $8\times 255 = 2040$\\
\botrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{Structural observations}\label{sec:obs}

\begin{observation}[keyless last layer]\label{obs:last}
The ciphertext is $C = F(x_{27}\oplus \rk_{27})$; no key is added after the last round. Hence
$\Finv(C) = y_{27} = x_{27}\oplus\rk_{27}$ can be computed by anyone.
\end{observation}

We confirmed this identity in code for 10\,000 random keys and plaintexts of each key size. It has two
consequences.

\emph{For DFA,} a fault in the S-box input of round 27 gives no key information, since the attacker can
compute $y_{27}$ and $y_{27}'$ directly and both are key-free. Faults must therefore be injected in
round 26 or earlier, and the attacker must invert at least one keyed round.

\emph{For round counting,} the 28-round cipher equals 27 keyed rounds, a final key addition $\rk_{27}$,
and a public bijection $F$. Any attack that works backwards from the ciphertext removes $F$ at no cost.
Round counts in cryptanalytic results on Dipper, including its security margin, should therefore be
read as 27 keyed nonlinear rounds plus a whitening key.

\paragraph{Splitting a round key into two groups.} Suppose the attacker knows
$w = y_T$ for a correct computation and $w' = y_T'$ for a faulty one, and guesses $K = \rk_T$. Then
$x_T = w\oplus K$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa}
P^{-1}(x_T) = P^{-1}(w)\oplus \kappa, \qquad \kappa = P^{-1}(K),
\end{equation}
because $P$ is a bit permutation. The words of $P^{-1}(x_T)$ are $(A_2,B_1,C_2,D_1)$ of round $T-1$.
By (\ref{eq:inv}) the words $A,B$ of $y_{T-1}$ depend only on $(\kappa_A,\kappa_B)$, and the words $C,D$
only on $(\kappa_C,\kappa_D)$. Guessing $\kappa$ instead of $K$ therefore splits the 64-bit guess into
two independent 32-bit groups.

\paragraph{Bit-serial structure of the subtraction.} Within a group, write $X$ for the mixed word
($A$ or $C$) and $Y$ for the other one. Bit $j$ of $X_1 = X_2\boxminus Y_1$ depends only on bits
$0,\dots,j$ of $X_2$ and $Y_1$, through the borrow into bit $j$. Since $X_2 = x\oplus\kappa_X$ and
$Y_1 = y\oplus\kappa_Y$ for known $x,y$, bit $j$ of $X_1$ is determined by key bits $0,\dots,j$ of the
group and one borrow bit carried from the previous position. This is what allows a guess of two key
bits per step.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:msb}
Consider a filter that only tests which nibbles of $\Delta y_{T-1}$ are non-zero (as under M1 and M3).
Then it cannot distinguish $\kappa$ from $\kappa\oplus\delta$ for any $\delta$ that is non-zero only in
the most significant bits of the four words. Hence at least 4 candidates remain per group, and at
least 16 per round key.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The test depends only on $\Delta X_1 = X_1\oplus X_1'$ and $\Delta Y_1 = Y_1\oplus Y_1'$, because the
rotations are linear and the inverse S-box maps non-zero differences to non-zero differences.
Flipping the top bit of $\kappa_X$ flips bit 15 of both $X_2$ and $X_2'$. This adds $2^{15}$ modulo
$2^{16}$ to both $X_1$ and $X_1'$, and adding $2^{15}$ only flips bit 15, so $\Delta X_1$ is unchanged.
Flipping the top bit of $\kappa_Y$ flips bit 15 of both $Y_1$ and $Y_1'$, so $\Delta Y_1$ is unchanged,
and it adds $2^{15}$ to both $X_1$ and $X_1'$, so $\Delta X_1$ is unchanged as well.
\end{proof}

Under M2 the filter also tests the value of the difference in the active nibble, and the proposition
does not apply; in our runs the M2 filter always reached a single candidate.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:zero}
For the variants \texttt{xor} ($A_2 = A_1\oplus B_1$, $C_2 = C_1\oplus D_1$) and \texttt{none}
($A_2 = A_1$, $C_2 = C_1$), a filter that only tests which nibbles of $\Delta y_{T-1}$ are non-zero
accepts every key guess whenever it accepts the correct one.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Without carries, $\Delta X_1 = \Delta X_2\oplus\Delta Y_1$ for \texttt{xor} and $\Delta X_1 = \Delta X_2$
for \texttt{none}. By (\ref{eq:kappa}), $\Delta X_2$ and $\Delta Y_1$ are words of
$P^{-1}(w\oplus w')$, in which the key cancels. Hence none of these differences depends on $\kappa$,
and the result of the test is the same for every guess.
\end{proof}

For Dipper the borrow chain makes $\Delta X_1$ depend on the key bits below each differing position.
This is the only source of key information for the filter of Section~\ref{sec:filter} under M1 and M3.

\paragraph{Redundant round-key words.} The key schedule makes some words of consecutive round keys
dependent. From (\ref{eq:ks96}), words 2 and 3 of $\rk_{27}$ are determined by words 0 and 1 of
$\rk_{26}$; from (\ref{eq:ks128}), words 0 and 1 of $\rk_{27}$ are determined by words 0 and 1 of
$\rk_{26}$. Candidates for consecutive round keys that violate these relations can be discarded
without any further fault.

\paragraph{Key-state recovery.} For the 96-bit key, $\rk_{26}$ and $\rk_{27}$ determine the whole key
state at round 26: $K_0,\dots,K_3$ are the words of $\rk_{26}$, $K_4$ is word 0 of $\rk_{27}$, and $K_5$ is
word 1 of $\rk_{27}$ with the inverse S-box on bits 7--4. For the 128-bit key they fix
$K_0,\dots,K_5$ ($K_4$ is word 2 of $\rk_{27}$ and $K_5$ is word 3 of $\rk_{27}$ with the inverse
S-box on bits 15--12); the remaining words follow from $\rk_{25}$: $K_6$ is word 2 of $\rk_{25}$ and
$K_7$ is word 3 of $\rk_{25}$ with the S-box on bits 15--12. Running the inverse update 26 times gives
the master key.

\section{The attack}\label{sec:attack}

\subsection{Overview}

The attack recovers round keys from the end of the cipher, one per stage (Algorithm~\ref{alg:chain}):
\begin{itemize}
\item stage 1: $\rk_{27}$ from faults in round 26;
\item stage 2: $\rk_{26}$ from faults in round 25;
\item stage 3 (64/128, option A only): $\rk_{25}$ from faults in round 24.
\end{itemize}
In each stage the attacker computes, for every fault, the values $w=y_T$ and $w'=y_T'$ by peeling the
ciphertexts with the round keys found so far (for $T=27$ by Observation~\ref{obs:last} alone). A
filter then keeps the guesses $K$ of $\rk_T$ for which
\begin{equation}\label{eq:check}
\Delta y_{T-1} = \Finv(w\oplus K)\oplus\Finv(w'\oplus K)
\end{equation}
fits the fault model. Because the fault is one round before the target key, we call this the
\emph{near} filter. When a stage ends, more than one candidate may remain; every surviving candidate
is carried into the next stage as a separate \emph{prefix}. Finally the candidates are turned into
master keys and tested with the known plaintext--ciphertext pair.

\subsection{The near filter}\label{sec:filter}

For each fault pair, the group that contains the fault is read off from $P^{-1}(w\oplus w')$: a fault
in one word of $y_{T-1}$ changes only the words of its own group in $(A_2,B_1,C_2,D_1)$. The pair is
then used only in that group.

Within a group the filter is a depth-first search over the 16 bit positions
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:filter}). At position $j$ it tries the four values of
$(\kappa_{X,j},\kappa_{Y,j})$ and, for every stored fault pair, computes bit $j$ of $X_1$ and $X_1'$
from the borrow left by position $j-1$. After the rotations, each nibble of $y_{T-1}$ consists of four
cyclically consecutive bits of $X_1$ or $Y_1$; for example the nibble $t$ of $A$ is made of bits
$4t+1,\dots,4t+4 \pmod{16}$ of $A_1$, since $A = A_1\ggg 1$. A nibble is known to be active as soon as
one of its bits differs between the correct and the faulty computation. A partial guess is discarded
as soon as one fault pair shows more active nibbles than the model allows (one for M1 and M2, two
adjacent ones in an aligned byte for M3). For M2 the value of the active nibble is also checked: when all
four of its bits are known, the inverse S-box is applied to both values and the difference must have
weight one. All fault pairs of a group are filtered jointly in one search, so the number of partial
guesses shrinks with every fault.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Near filter for one group $(X,Y)$ with fault pairs $(x_k,y_k,x_k',y_k')$, $k=1..n$}\label{alg:filter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Procedure{Search}{$j$, partial key $(\kappa_X,\kappa_Y)$ on bits $<j$, borrows $b_k,b_k'$}
\For{$(u,v)\in\{0,1\}^2$}
  \State set bit $j$ of $\kappa_X$ to $u$ and of $\kappa_Y$ to $v$
  \For{$k=1..n$}
    \State $X_2\gets x_k\oplus\kappa_X$, $Y_1\gets y_k\oplus\kappa_Y$ (same for the faulty values)
    \State compute bit $j$ of $X_1 = X_2\boxminus Y_1$ and the new borrow from $b_k$ (same for $b_k'$)
    \State mark nibbles of $y_{T-1}$ that have a differing known bit as active
    \If{active nibbles do not fit the model, or (M2) a complete active nibble has wrong weight}
      \State reject $(u,v)$ and continue with the next value
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{$j=15$} output $(\kappa_X,\kappa_Y)$ \Else\ \Call{Search}{$j+1$, \dots} \EndIf
\EndFor
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The search is exact: it accepts a guess if and only if the direct check (\ref{eq:check}) accepts it for
every fault pair of the group. We verified this by comparing the two on random inputs
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}). Only two key bits are guessed per step; the borrow of each fault pair is
computed, not guessed. To bound the running time, one call of the search is stopped after $10^{6}$
partial guesses (the \emph{node cap}); a group whose search hit the cap is treated as not finished and its
count as a lower bound.

\subsection{Stop rules}

The attacker does not know the key and must decide from the candidate counts alone when to stop
asking for faults. We use two rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Floor rule.} Stop a stage when, for every live prefix, both groups have at most $f$
survivors and neither search hit the node cap. The value $f$ is the smallest set the model can reach:
$f=4$ for M1 by Proposition~\ref{prop:msb}, $f=1$ for M2, and $f=8$ for M3
(Section~\ref{sec:secondary}).
\item \emph{Threshold rule $\theta$.} Stop a stage when no search hit the node cap and the total number
of candidate tuples over all prefixes is at most $2^{\theta}$. The remaining candidates are left to the
next stage and to the final check. We use $\theta = 8$ and $\theta = 12$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chaining the stages and recovering the master key}

At the end of a stage every combination of surviving group candidates of every live prefix becomes a
prefix of the next stage. A prefix peels the ciphertexts with its own round keys. If one of its round
keys is wrong, the computed values $w,w'$ are wrong as well; the prefix dies as soon as a fault pair
becomes impossible under it, either because the difference reaches both groups or because a group has
no survivor left.

After the last stage, each tuple of round keys gives a master key through the key-state recovery of
Section~\ref{sec:obs}. We keep only master keys whose re-expanded round keys reproduce all recovered
round keys (this applies the redundant-word relations), remove duplicates, and test the rest with the
known plaintext--ciphertext pair.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Full key recovery (64/96; 64/128 option A adds a stage for $\rk_{25}$)}\label{alg:chain}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathcal{P}\gets\{\varnothing\}$ \Comment{prefixes: candidate values of the round keys found so far}
\For{$T = 27, 26$}
  \Repeat
    \State obtain a correct/faulty ciphertext pair with a fault in round $T-1$
    \For{each prefix $p\in\mathcal{P}$}
      \State peel both ciphertexts with $p$ to get $w,w'$; drop $p$ if the pair is impossible
      \State add the pair to its group and rerun the near filter of that group
    \EndFor
  \Until{the stop rule holds}
  \State $\mathcal{P}\gets\{(p,\rk_T) : p\in\mathcal{P},\ \rk_T \text{ from the surviving group candidates of } p\}$
\EndFor
\For{each $p\in\mathcal{P}$}
  \State master key $M\gets$ inverse key schedule of $p$; keep $M$ if it reproduces $p$ and is new
  \State output $M$ if it encrypts the known plaintext to the known ciphertext
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The 128-bit key}

For the 128-bit key, $\rk_{26}$ and $\rk_{27}$ fix only 96 of the 128 state bits. We measured two
options:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Option A} recovers $\rk_{25}$ as a third stage (faults in round 24) and then the master key as
above;
\item \emph{Option B} stops after $\rk_{26}$ and $\rk_{27}$ and searches the $2^{32}$ values of the
missing words $(K_6,K_7)$ of the round-26 state. For each value the master key is obtained by the
inverse update, and the known ciphertext is decrypted round by round while the round keys are produced,
until the plaintext is reached and compared.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The deep filter for the variants}\label{sec:deep}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:zero}, the near filter learns nothing about the variants under M1 and M3. For
them we used a \emph{deep} filter, with the fault two rounds before the target key ($T-2$). Since the
mixing layer of the variants is linear, an S-box output difference $d$ of the faulted nibble (or byte,
or bit) at round $T-2$ produces the exact difference $P(\mathrm{ARX}(d))$ at $y_{T-1}$, because the round
key $\rk_{T-1}$ cancels. Each possible $d$ is a \emph{hypothesis}; M1 gives $16\times 15=240$ hypotheses.
The deep filter guesses $\kappa$ bit by bit as before and keeps, for every fault pair, the set of
hypotheses that are still consistent with the nibbles of $y_{T-1}$ computed so far; a guess dies when this
set becomes empty for some pair. The two groups are filtered separately, which is a relaxation: the
true key always survives, and wrong combinations are removed later by the next stage or by the final
check.

The deep filter needs a linear mixing layer, so it was not applied to Dipper. For Dipper the difference
after the addition depends on the carries, that is on unknown state values, and the hypotheses would
not give exact differences. We did not build such a filter. As a consequence, under M1 and M3 Dipper and
the variants are attacked with faults in different rounds (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Complexity}

The work of a stage is the number of partial guesses evaluated by the filter, summed over all faults
and all prefixes. Each partial guess costs one bit operation per stored fault pair. Table~\ref{tab:stage}
reports this number together with the CPU time. The final check costs one key-schedule inversion and one
encryption per candidate; option B adds $2^{32}$ such operations.

\subsection{The 128-bit key}\label{sec:optB}

Option A needs one stage more than 64/96 and about 47\% more faults under the floor rule (mean 332.0

needs a median of 208 faults and a $2^{32}$ search, against 316 faults for option A under the floor rule or
137 faults for option A with $\theta = 12$.

\paragraph{Same attack, nibble faults.} Under M1 the near filter, which recovers the Dipper key, gives no
information at all on the variants (Proposition~\ref{prop:zero}; confirmed by $2\times10^{6}$ random guesses
per variant, all accepted). In Dipper, the borrow chain of the subtraction makes the activity pattern at the
fault point depend on the key, and this dependence is exactly what the filter uses. The same holds for M3.

\paragraph{Same attack, bit faults.} Under M2 the near filter also uses the value of the difference after
the inverse S-box, and it works for all three ciphers. Here Dipper needed fewer faults: a median of 115
against 299 for both variants. The two variants behave almost identically (means 307.9 and 308.2). In
Table~\ref{tab:surv} the M2 curves of the variants stay above Dipper's after 10 faults.

\paragraph{Different attacks, nibble faults.} With faults one round earlier, the deep filter recovers the
key of the variants with a median of 81 (\texttt{xor}) and 145 (\texttt{none}) faults, fewer than Dipper
needs with the near filter (210). This comparison is between two different attacks, and the deep filter
uses the linearity of the variants' mixing layer. A deep attack on Dipper would have to handle carries in
the propagated difference; we did not build one, so we cannot say how many faults it would need.

\paragraph{Summary.} In none of our experiments did the modular addition increase the number of faults
needed. Its main effect was to make a fault one round before the target key informative under nibble and
byte faults, which gives the attacker a check one round closer to the ciphertext.

a similar purpose, keeping the effect of the carries exact while guessing only two key bits per step.

GIFT-64 has the same S-box and bit permutation as Dipper but a different key addition and no modular
addition. The GIFT-64 figure of Xie et al.\ (31 faults with a $2^{16}$ search) is lower than ours for Dipper
(a median of 91 faults with $2^{12}$ candidates left), but the fault positions and stop criteria differ, and
we did not run their attack under our conditions. The automated framework of Saha et al.~\cite{Saha2018}
reports attacks on GIFT with one or two nibble faults and a remaining key space that must be searched; the
two versions of that paper give different figures, and we do not compare with them directly. DFA results on the ARX cipher SPECK~\cite{Tupsamudre2014,Huo2015} report much smaller
numbers of faults for the last round key, but SPECK's round structure differs strongly from Dipper's, and those
figures concern a single round key.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the modular addition does.} For DFA the additions have two effects in our
measurements. First, they make the
pattern of active nibbles after a fault depend on the key, which is what allows a fault one round before
the target key to be used at all under nibble and byte faults. Second, under bit faults the same near attack
needed fewer faults on Dipper than on the variants. Neither effect increased the cost of the attack.

\paragraph{Why so many faults are needed.} A fault affects only one of the two 32-bit groups of a round key,
so on average half of the faults are spent on each group. Within a group, a fault constrains the key bits only
up to the positions where its differences propagate through the borrow chain, so the high bits of a group are
learned only from some of the faults. In the worked example (Table~\ref{tab:example}) the candidate counts stay
large for the first 25 faults and then fall quickly. The threshold rule avoids paying for the last few bits.

\paragraph{Round counting.} By Observation~\ref{obs:last}, the last round of Dipper ends with a public
bijection. This does not affect the DFA results above, which count faults, but it matters when the number of
rounds attacked in other cryptanalytic results is compared with the 28 rounds of the cipher.

\paragraph{Limitations.} All faults are simulated, with exactly the stated models; real faults may affect more
bits, several rounds, or the control flow. The attacker is assumed to know the round of the fault. We studied
only the attacks described here: in particular, a deep attack on Dipper that handles carries, and attacks that
use faults in the key schedule, were not studied. The node cap of $10^{6}$ partial guesses can delay a stop
decision but cannot make the attack accept a wrong key; the final plaintext--ciphertext test always found
exactly one key, the correct one. CPU times were measured on a shared virtual machine and are indicative only.

%=====================================================================
\section{Countermeasures}\label{sec:cm}

The attack needs pairs of correct and faulty outputs for the same input. The standard protection is
therefore to detect the fault before the ciphertext is released: compute the encryption twice, in time or in
space, and output the result only if the two agree, or check the result by decrypting
it~\cite{BarEl2006,Barenghi2012,Karaklajic2013}. These schemes stop the attack described here as long as the
fault does not affect both computations in the same way. Infective countermeasures take a different approach:
instead of suppressing a faulty output they make it useless to the attacker, for example by randomising
it~\cite{Gierlichs2012}.

Their main limit is the common-mode fault: when both computations share hardware, code or data, a single fault
can change both in the same way, and the comparison passes. Our related study~\cite{Huseynli2027} examines which
faults in shared resources remain undetected under duplication-based protection in lightweight block cipher
implementations.

A cheaper option is to protect only the last rounds. The attacks measured here used faults in rounds 24--26
against Dipper (23--25 for the variants with the deep filter), but faults in earlier rounds were not studied, so
we make no claim on how many rounds must be protected. We did not evaluate any countermeasure experimentally.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In software simulation, a nibble-fault DFA recovered the full Dipper key in all 1000 trials for both key sizes.
For the 96-bit key it needed a median of 210 faulty ciphertexts when 16 candidates remain, and 116 or 91 faults
when $2^{8}$ or $2^{12}$ candidates are left for a final check. For the 128-bit key it needed a median of 208 faults
and a $2^{32}$ search, or 316 faults without a search. The modular addition is what makes the round-26 fault
informative under nibble and byte faults, and in our measurements it did not increase the number of faults needed
compared with variants without it. Four bits of each round key cannot be found by the filter under nibble or byte
faults, but they were resolved by the next stage in every trial. Dipper's last round ends with a keyless, invertible
layer, so its 28 rounds amount to 27 keyed nonlinear rounds plus a whitening key.

Future work includes a deep fault attack on Dipper that handles carries, faults in the key schedule, and
experiments with physical fault injection.
